## Learning goals [#goals]

After this lesson you should be able to:

1. Describe the return of an investment with a scenario table: the possible outcomes and their probabilities.
2. Compute the expected return, the variance and the standard deviation, and state that the unit of variance is %² and the unit of standard deviation is %.
3. Compute the covariance and the correlation coefficient, and explain that correlation is about whether the **deviations from each expected value** have the same direction, not about whether returns are positive.
4. ★ Derive the variance of a linear combination of two random variables, and use it to compute the risk of a two-asset portfolio.
5. Explain diversification: **as long as $\rho < 1$**, the portfolio standard deviation is less than the weighted average of the assets' standard deviations; when $\rho = 0$, the benefit comes from "variances add, standard deviations do not", not from the covariance term.

The portfolio variance formula in this lesson is the core of <Ref lesson="4.2" />.

## Random variables and scenario tables [#random-variables]

A quantity whose value is uncertain, but whose possible values and their probabilities are known, is called a <Def id="random-variable">random variable</Def>. The return of an investment is a random variable.

Here is an example: the annual returns (%) of stock A and bond fund B in three economic scenarios.

| Scenario | Probability $p$ | Stock A | Bond fund B |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Boom | 0.25 | 30 | 1 |
| Normal | 0.50 | 10 | 5 |
| Recession | 0.25 | −10 | 3 |

The probabilities of all scenarios must add up to 1. In reality we do not know the true probabilities; this table is a simplified model. How to estimate these quantities from historical data, and how large the estimation error can be, is covered in <Ref lesson="0.4" />.

## Expected value [#expected-value]

The <Def id="expected-value">expected value</Def> is the average result if the same situation is repeated many times. It is computed as a probability-weighted average:

$$
E[R] = \sum_i p_i R_i
$$

In the example, $E[R_A] = 10\%$ and $E[R_B] = 3.5\%$.

Note: the expected value is not necessarily a result that can actually occur in a given year (one of A's three possible outcomes happens to be 10, but none of B's outcomes is 3.5), and it is not a forecast for next year.

## Variance and standard deviation [#variance]

The expected value alone is not enough: two investments can have the same expected value but very different spreads of outcomes. We want to measure how far the outcomes are from the expected value. Deviations can be positive or negative, and a plain average of them is always 0, so we square them first and then take the probability-weighted average. This gives the <Def id="variance">variance</Def>. The square root of the variance is the <Def id="standard-deviation">standard deviation</Def>:

$$
\text{Var}(R) = \sum_i p_i \big(R_i - E[R]\big)^2, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(R)}
$$

- A: $\text{Var} = 200$ (%²), $\sigma_A \approx 14.14\%$.
- B: $\text{Var} = 2.75$ (%²), $\sigma_B \approx 1.66\%$.

**Units**: the unit of variance is %², which has no intuitive meaning. The standard deviation is back in %, so it can be compared directly with the expected return. "Volatility" in finance means the standard deviation. Also note that squaring makes large deviations count more: a deviation of 20 contributes 100 times as much as a deviation of 2.

## Covariance and correlation [#covariance]

The risk of a portfolio depends not only on how much each asset varies, but also on whether the assets vary together. This is measured by the <Def id="covariance">covariance</Def>:

$$
\text{Cov}(R_A, R_B) = \sum_i p_i \big(R_{A,i} - E[R_A]\big)\big(R_{B,i} - E[R_B]\big)
$$

In a scenario where both are **above** or both are **below their own expected values**, the product is positive. If one is above and the other is below, the product is negative.

| Scenario | $p$ | A deviation | B deviation | Product |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Boom | 0.25 | +20 | −2.5 | −50 |
| Normal | 0.50 | 0 | +1.5 | 0 |
| Recession | 0.25 | −20 | −0.5 | +10 |

The probability-weighted sum is $\text{Cov} = 0.25 \times (-50) + 0.5 \times 0 + 0.25 \times 10 = -10$ (%²).

The size of the covariance depends on the units, so it is hard to compare. Dividing it by the two standard deviations gives the <Def id="correlation">correlation coefficient</Def>:

$$
\rho_{AB} = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_A, R_B)}{\sigma_A \sigma_B} \approx \frac{-10}{14.14 \times 1.66} \approx -0.43
$$

The correlation coefficient has no unit and lies in $[-1, 1]$. It measures only linear relationships, and correlation does not imply causation (<Ref concept="correlation-vs-causation" />).

**Key point: correlation is about the direction of deviations, not about whether returns are positive.** Suppose two funds both have an expected return of 8%, and in one year one fund rises 15% and the other rises 2%. Both "went up", but one is above its expected value and the other is below, so this year contributes a negative amount to the covariance. "Both went up, so they are positively correlated" is wrong reasoning. Also, a single observation cannot show a probabilistic relationship.

## Expected value and variance of a linear combination [#linear-combination]

Now split your money into two parts: a fraction $w$ in A and a fraction $1 - w$ in B. The portfolio return is $R_P = wR_A + (1-w)R_B$.

The expected value is linear:

$$
E[R_P] = w\,E[R_A] + (1-w)\,E[R_B]
$$

But the variance is **not** linear. The <Def id="portfolio-variance">portfolio variance</Def> has an extra covariance term:

$$
\text{Var}(R_P) = w^2\sigma_A^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_B^2 + 2w(1-w)\,\text{Cov}(R_A, R_B)
$$

Example: 50% in A and 50% in B.

- $E[R_P] = 0.5 \times 10 + 0.5 \times 3.5 = 6.75\%$.
- $\text{Var}(R_P) = 0.25 \times 200 + 0.25 \times 2.75 + 2 \times 0.25 \times (-10) = 45.69$ (%²), so $\sigma_P \approx 6.76\%$.
- Check: the portfolio returns in the three scenarios are 15.5, 7.5 and −3.5. Computing the variance directly from these three numbers gives the same result.

The weighted average of the standard deviations is $0.5 \times 14.14 + 0.5 \times 1.66 = 7.90\%$, but the actual portfolio standard deviation is only $6.76\%$. Why is it smaller? That is the topic of the next section. The derivation of this formula is left as <Ref check="Q5" />.

## Diversification [#diversification]

Rewrite the portfolio variance with the correlation coefficient:

$$
\sigma_P^2 = w^2\sigma_A^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_B^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B
$$

Look at three cases.

**$\rho = 1$**: the right side is a perfect square, and $\sigma_P = w\sigma_A + (1-w)\sigma_B$. The portfolio standard deviation equals the weighted average, so there is no benefit.

**$\rho = 0$**: the covariance term is 0, but

$$
\sigma_P = \sqrt{w^2\sigma_A^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_B^2} < w\sigma_A + (1-w)\sigma_B
$$

because $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} < a + b$ for positive $a, b$ (after squaring both sides, the right side has an extra $2ab$). So in this case the benefit comes from the fact that **variances add, but standard deviations do not**. It does not come from the covariance term. Compare a right triangle: the hypotenuse is shorter than the sum of the other two sides. Two uncorrelated sources of variation are like two perpendicular directions.

**The general case**: the covariance term only changes the size of the benefit. When $\rho > 0$ the benefit is smaller; when $\rho < 0$ it is larger; when $\rho = 1$ it is exactly 0. When $\rho = -1$ you can choose a weight that makes $\sigma_P = 0$ (<Ref check="Q6" />).

Conclusion: **as long as $\rho < 1$, there is a benefit from <Def id="diversification">diversification</Def>. Diversification does not require negative correlation.** In reality, the correlation between most assets is between 0 and 1, so diversification helps but cannot remove all risk (<Ref concept="systematic-risk" />).

A consequence: if you hold only the low-volatility asset B, adding a small amount of a **more volatile** asset A that is not highly correlated with B can lower the volatility of the portfolio.

## Summary [#summary]

- Expected value: the probability-weighted average. Variance: the probability-weighted average of squared deviations (%²). Standard deviation: the square root of the variance (%).
- Covariance: positive when the deviations from each expected value have the same direction, negative when they have opposite directions. Correlation coefficient: the covariance scaled to $[-1, 1]$. It is about deviations, not about whether returns are positive.
- The expected value of a portfolio is a weighted average; the variance of a portfolio has an extra covariance term and is **not** a weighted average.
- As long as $\rho < 1$ there is a benefit from diversification; at $\rho = 0$ the benefit comes from the fact that variances add but standard deviations do not.
- When you hold many assets with equal weights, the portfolio variance approaches the average covariance between the assets: the risk specific to each stock can be diversified away, but the common risk cannot (<Ref check="Q7" />).
